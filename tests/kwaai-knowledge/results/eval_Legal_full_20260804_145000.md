### q01 — What was the central legal question in Marbury v. Madison, and what doctrine did it establish?

The central legal question in Marbury v. Madison (1803) was whether a writ of mandamus could be issued by the Supreme Court to compel James Madison, Secretary of State, to deliver commissions to William Marbury and others.

The court established the doctrine that:

* The President does not have the authority to remove appointees once they have been appointed.
* Once an appointment has been made, it cannot be annulled by the Executive (in this case, the President).
* Appointees have a vested right to their office once they have been appointed and confirmed by the Senate (implied, although not explicitly stated in the excerpts).

The court also established that:

* The Supreme Court has the power of judicial review, which allows it to declare laws or actions of the Executive unconstitutional.
* In this case, the court found that a provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 was unconstitutional because it gave the Supreme Court the power to issue writs of mandamus in cases not specified by law.

The doctrine established by Marbury v. Madison is a cornerstone of American constitutional law, and it has had significant implications for the relationship between the Executive branch and the judiciary.

### q04 — What racial doctrine did Plessy v. Ferguson establish, and how did Brown v. Board of Education explicitly overturn it decades later?

According to [2] and [17], Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) established the "separate but equal" doctrine, which permitted separate schools for white and colored children provided that the facilities were equal.

Brown v. Board of Education (1954), as stated in [1], [3], [9], and [19]-[20], explicitly overturned this doctrine by holding that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." The court concluded that segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race deprives children of the minority group of equal educational opportunities, even though physical facilities and other "tangible" factors may be equal.

By doing so, Brown v. Board of Education made clear that the "separate but equal" doctrine had no place in the field of public education, as stated in [19]-[20].
