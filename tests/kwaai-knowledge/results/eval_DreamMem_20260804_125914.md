## Answers

### q01 — What is the Overfitted Brain Hypothesis, and who proposed it?

The Overfitted Brain Hypothesis (OBH) proposes that dreams help mitigate overfitting in the brain by providing a biologically-realistic "noise injection" through stochastic percolation of signals. The OBH states that the brain's tendency to overfit, or become too specialized and lose generalizability, is similar to an artificial neural network's propensity for overfitting.

The provided sources do not explicitly state who proposed the Overfitted Brain Hypothesis.

### q02 — According to Hoel's paper, what evolutionary function do dreams serve in relation to machine-learning-style overfitting?

[6] states "That is, dreams are a biological mechanism for increasing generalizability via the creation of corrupted sensory inputs from stochastic activity across the hierarchy of neural structures." 

[20] also mentions this same idea: "This is the Overﬁtted Brian Hypothesis (OBH). The goal of this paper is to formally ﬁll-out the OBH by investigating the evidence that the brain ﬁts to a data set composed of the statistically self-similar daily experiences of the organism, while nightly dreams improve the generality and robustness of an animal’s representations, cognition, and perceptual systems, by generating data far outside the organism’s daily 'training set' in a warped or corrupted way (Hoel, 2019)."

Both sources suggest that Hoel's paper supports the idea that dreams serve to increase generalizability by creating corrupted sensory inputs from stochastic activity.

### q03 — What is the central argument of Poe's "Sleep Is for Forgetting," and what companion paper does it explicitly pair with in the Journal of Neuroscience?

According to excerpt [11], Gina Poe's central argument in her article "Sleep Is for Forgetting" (Poe • Sleep Is for Forgetting J. Neurosci., January 18, 2017 • 37(3):464 – 473) is that memories are acquired and maintained by constant synaptic pruning, and REM sleep provides ideal conditions for this process.

According to excerpt [20], the companion paper explicitly paired with it in the Journal of Neuroscience is a paper titled "Sleep-induced changes in associative memory" by Stickgold et al. (1999), cited as Stickgold, R., Scott, L., Rittenhouse, C., and Hobson, J. A. (1999).

### q04 — What dual role do sleep oscillations play according to "Remembering to Forget: A Dual Role for Sleep Oscillations in Memory Consolidation and Forgetting"?

According to [9], [10], [14], and [18], sleep oscillations, particularly non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep rhythms, play a dual role:

1. "download/save" important information: co-opting offline brain oscillations to simultaneously save important, useful information.
2. "delete/erase" noisy, un-adaptive information: the same oscillations used for memory consolidation can also operate to erase select memories.

This dual role is described in [18] as "remembering to forget".

### q05 — What does "A model of autonomous interactions between hippocampus and neocortex" propose regarding sleep-dependent memory consolidation?

According to [20], a computational model that simulates interactions between hippocampal and neocortical memory systems proposes the following regarding sleep-dependent memory consolidation:

1. The hippocampus actively helps build neocortical semantic representations during sleep (see [18] for core ideas adopted from several perspectives).
2. Information stored initially in the hippocampus can help to build neocortical representations during sleep.
3. The alternation of different stages of sleep across the night facilitates graceful integration of new information with existing knowledge.

Overall, this model views sleep-dependent memory consolidation as a process that updates internal models of the world by restructuring and integrating new information into existing knowledge over time.

### q06 — According to "Autobiographical memory and hyperassociativity in the dreaming brain," what implications does hyperassociativity during dreaming have for memory?

[17] states that hyperassociativity in the dreaming brain can provide insight into the nature of memory consolidation, as activated memories within the sleeping brain reflect one's personal life history (autobiography).
